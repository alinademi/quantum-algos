<Depth level="kid">
Here is a puzzle. Fill a 3 by 3 grid with +1 and −1. Each row must multiply to +1, each column to −1. Try it: a rule always breaks. By rows, all nine numbers multiply to +1. By columns, to −1. Both cannot be true.

Now take two qubits and nine measurements, one per square, each giving +1 or −1. Quantum theory says: measure a row's three together, and the results always multiply to +1. A column's always multiply to −1.

So the qubits cannot carry a grid of answers, ready before you ask. An answer set in advance would have to change with the row or column you measure it with.

In a game, one friend gets a row and fills it so it multiplies to +1. The other gets a column and fills it so it multiplies to −1. No talking. They win if they agree on the square they share. Any plan made in advance loses at least 1 round in 9 on average; friends sharing two entangled pairs can win every round.
</Depth>

<Depth level="student">
The puzzle: fill a 3×3 grid with +1 and −1 so each row multiplies to +1 and each column to −1. It cannot be done: by rows, all nine numbers multiply to +1; by columns, to −1.

Now put a two-qubit [observable](/learn/observables/) with results +1 or −1 in each square, like X⊗X: X on the first qubit, X on the second. A row's three can be measured together, and quantum theory says their results always multiply to +1, in any state. A column's always multiply to −1.

Fixed results, one per square and the same in its row and its column, would solve the puzzle, so none exist. This is contextuality, the subject of the Kochen-Specker theorem. One system shows it; no distance is needed.

In the game, Alice gets a row and Bob a column, with no talking. Two players far apart make this a test anyone can run: Alice's answer cannot depend on Bob's column. A fixed plan wins at most 8 rounds in 9 on average; ideal players sharing two entangled pairs win every round.
</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">
The Mermin-Peres square, in de Wolf's version, holds nine two-qubit observables $O_{rc}$:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
X \otimes X & Y \otimes Z & Z \otimes Y \\
Y \otimes Y & Z \otimes X & X \otimes Z \\
Z \otimes Z & X \otimes Y & Y \otimes X
\end{array}
$$

Each $O_{rc}$ is Hermitian with $O_{rc}^2 = I$, so its eigenvalues are $\pm 1$. Two Pauli products commute when they anticommute in an even number of slots, and entries of one line differ in both slots. So each row and column is a context: a commuting set, measured jointly. Since $XYZ = iI$ and $XZY = -iI$, row 1 is $(XYZ) \otimes (XZY) = I$ and column 1 is $(XYZ) \otimes (XYZ) = -I$; every row gives $+I$, every column $-I$. In any state, a row's results multiply to $+1$, a column's to $-1$.

Noncontextual values $v(O_{rc}) = \pm 1$ that obey the product rule $v(AB) = v(A)\,v(B)$ for commuting $A, B$ would make $\prod_{r,c} v(O_{rc})$ both $+1$ (by rows) and $-1$ (by columns). No such values exist: a state-independent Kochen-Specker proof in $\mathbb{C}^4$. Kochen and Specker's original used 117 yes-no observables (directions) in real 3D space, $\mathbb{R}^3$. Hidden-variable theories that match quantum theory must be contextual. Bohmian mechanics, for one, fixes only positions; other results depend on the whole measurement setup.

The game adds distance, so Alice's value for cell $(x, y)$ cannot depend on $y$. Asked row $x$ and column $y$, they win if her answers $a$ multiply to $+1$, his answers $b$ to $-1$, and $a_y = b_x$. A plan is two sheets. If one breaks a rule, that line's three asks lose; if not, the sheets differ somewhere. Either way $P_\text{win} \le 8/9$, and shared randomness only mixes plans. Quantum players share two singlets $|\psi^-\rangle = (|01\rangle - |10\rangle)/\sqrt2$, each made from $|11\rangle$ by $H$ then CNOT; each player holds one qubit of each pair. As $\langle \psi^- | \sigma \otimes \sigma | \psi^- \rangle = -1$ for $\sigma = X, Y, Z$, the shared cell gives $\langle O_A O_B \rangle = (-1)^2 = 1$: they agree, and $P_\text{win} = 1$.
</Depth>
