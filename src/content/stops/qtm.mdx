<Depth level="kid">

Imagine a long strip of squares, each holding a symbol, and a reader that can move one square left or right. A rule tells the reader how to change its own setting, what to write and where to move next. In the quantum version, the state has wave amplitudes for different possible arrangements of the strip and reader. Later operations can make contributions reinforce or cancel. This was the first general universal quantum machine model. A circuit can work through the local reading and writing rules, and the reader can follow a circuit’s instructions one by one. They have the same power with reasonable extra work. Our tiny four-position ring illustrates only motion, not an entire machine. Real models must preserve the quantum evolution of the entire tape.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Deutsch’s 1985 paper introduced the first universal quantum Turing machine. A configuration specifies the internal state, head position and tape symbols. Transition amplitudes link configurations according to a unitary rule. The lab fixes the tape symbols and uses a head moving on a four-position ring, with a two-state direction control. This is a quantum walk, not a universal machine. A local update changes the cell under the head and moves the head. A uniform [circuit](/learn/universal/) can simulate those updates over the reachable cells, and a universal QTM can execute the circuit’s gates. The overhead is polynomial, so their [asymptotic](/learn/big-o/) computational power agrees. Measuring the head still yields just one position per run.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A QTM has configurations $|q,h,t\rangle$ and a local transition rule whose global evolution must be unitary; arbitrary normalized outgoing amplitudes alone do not ensure this. Deutsch (1985) introduced the first universal QTM. Its transition is local: only the scanned cell, internal state and head position change. Over T steps only O(T) tape cells beyond the input can be reached. Encode the tape and head in qubits, and simulate local rules on the reachable region by uniform circuits of polynomial size ([Yao, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1109/SFCS.1993.366852)). Conversely, a universal QTM can simulate a uniformly described circuit gate by gate to the required accuracy with polynomial overhead (Bernstein and Vazirani, 1997). This toy step is H on the internal coin followed by a reversible conditional cyclic shift. Position probabilities are obtained by summing $|a_{c,h}|^2$ over both coin values c. At three steps from coin 0 and position 0, contributions interfere so position 3 has probability one despite two classically reachable positions.

</Depth>
