<Depth level="kid">

Imagine a long strip of squares, each holding a symbol, and a reader that can move one square left or right. A rule tells the reader how to change its own setting, what to write and where to move next. In the quantum version, the state has wave amplitudes for different possible arrangements of the strip and reader. Later operations can make contributions reinforce or cancel. This was an early formal model of a universal quantum computer; it has the same computational power as the circuit model when resources are compared fairly. Our tiny four-position ring illustrates only motion, not an entire machine. Real models must preserve the quantum evolution of the entire tape.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Deutsch's 1985 paper introduced a universal quantum Turing machine, an early formal universal quantum computer model. A configuration specifies the internal state, head position and tape symbols. Transition amplitudes link configurations according to a unitary rule. The lab fixes the tape symbols and uses a head moving on a four-position ring, with a two-state direction control. This is a quantum walk, not a universal machine. Properly defined QTMs and uniform [universal circuits](/learn/universal/) simulate each other with polynomial overhead in standard models, meaning their [asymptotic](/learn/big-o/) computational power agrees. Measuring the head still yields just one position per run.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A QTM has configurations $|q,h,t\rangle$ and a local transition rule whose global evolution must be unitary; arbitrary normalized outgoing amplitudes alone do not ensure this. Uniform circuits and suitably defined QTMs simulate each other with polynomial overhead in standard complexity models ([Yao, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1109/SFCS.1993.366852)). This toy step is H on the internal coin followed by a reversible conditional cyclic shift.

</Depth>
