<Depth level="kid">

Imagine three workbenches and a list of tasks. Some tasks use one bench, some need two at the same time. If two tasks use different benches, the workers can finish them together. If they share a bench, they have to take turns. Count the total benches, the tasks and the rounds: these are three different measures of the work. You can also count only particular expensive tasks, such as those connecting benches or needing special equipment. This picture is about scheduling, not the quantum state itself. Real quantum hardware may have extra restrictions and tasks may take different amounts of time, so fewer rounds do not always mean a shorter physical runtime.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

The width of a [circuit](/learn/circuits/) is the number of qubit wires allocated. Gate count is the number of operations. Depth counts sequential layers when gates on disjoint wires may run together. For example two H gates on different wires fit in one layer, but a later CNOT depends on both. Counting CNOT and T separately matters because they can be more expensive than simple local gates on some architectures. Compare a serial schedule with the parallel one in the lab: width and gate count stay unchanged while depth may shrink. These measures help describe how a resource grows with input size ([asymptotic cost](/learn/big-o/)); real hardware time requires durations and constraints.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

For a fixed ordered list of nonempty wire sets, assign each gate the earliest layer later than the last layer on any of its wires. The maximum assigned layer is the depth for that dependency ordering and unit-duration model. Commutation-based rewrites can change this value. Hardware duration, connectivity, measurement, classical latency and error are separate costs.

</Depth>
