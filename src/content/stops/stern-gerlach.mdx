<Depth level="kid">

Shoot silver atoms, one at a time, through a magnet whose pull is stronger near one pole. Each atom acts like a tiny magnet, so the uneven pull pushes it up or down, depending on how it points.

Tiny compass needles could point any way, so they would smear out in a line on the screen. The atoms don't. Each one lands in one of just two spots, up or down, never in between.

Now keep only the up atoms and send them through a second magnet. If it stands upright, they all go up again. Turn it on its side, and half go left, half go right. Keep one side, then add a third, upright magnet: half come out down! The sideways magnet wiped out the first answer.

Here the compass picture breaks: a needle can point any way, but an atom only ever answers up or down.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

In 1922 Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent silver atoms through an uneven magnetic field. The spin of each atom's one unpaired electron makes it a tiny magnet, so the field pushes it along the magnet's axis. Spin was proposed only in 1925; it explains the two spots.

Classical physics expected a smear. The screen showed two spots: spin up or spin down. This spin is a [qubit](/learn/qubit/): up is 0, down is 1.

Put magnets in a row. Magnet 1 is upright (Z) and lets only the up beam through, so each atom starts as 0.

- Magnet 2 upright: 100% up again.
- Magnet 2 on its side (X): 50% each way.
- Magnet 2 on its side, keeping one beam, then upright magnet 3: 50% up, 50% down, though magnet 1 blocked every down atom.

Turn magnet 2 by θ: up has amplitude cos(θ/2), so probability cos²(θ/2). That is 75% at 60° and 0% at 180°.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

With the field and its gradient both along $z$, a magnetic moment feels

$$
\vec{F} = \nabla(\vec{\mu} \cdot \vec{B}) \approx \mu_z \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}\,\hat{z}
$$

Silver has one unpaired $5s$ electron and no orbital angular momentum, so $\mu_z = -g\mu_B S_z/\hbar$ with $g \approx 2$. So spin up is pushed toward the weaker field. The tool's arrow points the way spin up goes, not along the gradient. Classical moments pointing every way would make a smear; two spots mean $S_z = \pm\hbar/2$. Spin one-half is a [qubit](/learn/qubit/): $|0\rangle = |{\uparrow_z}\rangle$ and $|1\rangle = |{\downarrow_z}\rangle$.

A magnet turned by $\theta$ about the beam (along $y$) has axis $\hat{n} = (\sin\theta, 0, \cos\theta)$ and measures $\hat{n} \cdot \vec{\sigma}$, whose eigenstates are

$$
|{\uparrow_n}\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle, \qquad |{\downarrow_n}\rangle = -\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle
$$

Started as $|0\rangle$ (the up beam of a $Z$ magnet), the atom goes up with amplitude $\langle{\uparrow_n}|0\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}$, so with probability $\cos^2\tfrac{\theta}{2}$. In general, a spin up along $\hat{m}$ goes up along $\hat{n}$ with probability $(1 + \hat{m} \cdot \hat{n})/2$.

Each magnet leaves its kept beam in that eigenstate, so only the last result matters. The down beam of an $X$ magnet is $(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ up to a global phase, and a $Z$ magnet then gives each result with probability $|1/\sqrt{2}|^2 = \tfrac12$. No state has a definite value for both, since $[\sigma_z, \sigma_x] = 2i\sigma_y \neq 0$.

Oven atoms are unpolarized, the [mixed state](/learn/mixed-states/) $\rho = I/2$, so the first magnet splits them $\tfrac12$ each way along any axis. Turning a magnet is [measuring in another basis](/learn/bases/).

</Depth>
