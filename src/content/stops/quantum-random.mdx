<Depth level="kid">

Flip a coin: heads or tails. Computer programs often use a recipe instead. It starts from a number called the seed and repeats the same steps. The answers look jumbled, but the same seed always gives the same list. Know the seed, and you can guess every answer.

A qubit can flip a quantum coin. Start it as 0, then a Hadamard gate gives it equal amplitudes for 0 and 1, so a measurement gives either one with equal chance. Measure it to get one bit. No seed and no recipe picks that bit ahead of time.

The coin picture breaks here. A tossed coin only seems random: if you knew exactly how it was thrown, you could work out how it lands. In quantum theory, a qubit has no hidden detail like that.

The qubit on this page is pretend: your computer makes its flips, not a real qubit.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Start a qubit as 0 and apply a Hadamard gate. Now it has amplitude $1/\sqrt{2}$ for 0 and $1/\sqrt{2}$ for 1. Measure it, and each result has probability $|1/\sqrt{2}|^2 = 1/2$. That is one fair random bit. Measuring the same qubit again repeats the bit, so each new bit needs a fresh qubit, or the same one reset to 0.

A pseudo-random generator is a program. It starts from a seed and applies one fixed rule over and over. Its bits look fair, but the seed decides them all: same seed, same bits.

You cannot tell the two apart by looking at the bits. The difference is how they are made. Real quantum devices have small flaws, so their raw bits get cleaned up before use (see [Extracting and testing randomness](/learn/randomness-extraction/)).

The qubit on this page is simulated. Its draws come from your browser's random number generator: a program, not a qubit.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Prepare $|0\rangle$ and apply $H$:

$$
H|0\rangle = |+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\big).
$$

A standard basis measurement gives $b \in \{0,1\}$ with probability $|\langle b|+\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$ and leaves the qubit in $|b\rangle$, so measuring again repeats $b$. Fresh qubits give independent bits: each $n$-bit string has probability $2^{-n}$.

A pseudo-random generator is a fixed map from a seed. The tool's program is a linear congruential generator,

$$
X_{k+1} = (aX_k + c) \bmod m,
$$

with $a = 1103515245$, $c = 12345$, $m = 2^{31}$, seed $X_0$, and output bit $\lfloor X_k / 2^{30} \rfloor$ for $k \ge 1$. Its $a$ and $c$ are those of the sample `rand()` in the C standard: a weak, teaching-only choice, since each $X_k$ is the whole state and one full output predicts all later ones. These constants give the full period $2^{31}$, but any deterministic generator with finite memory repeats at some point, and every bit is a function of $X_0$. Statistical tests judge only the output. A quantum generator's claim rests on the process: in quantum theory, $|+\rangle$ fixes only the odds, not the outcome.

In a classic device, one photon meets a balanced beam splitter with a detector on each path. Unequal detector efficiencies add bias, dead time adds correlations and dark counts add clicks with no photon, so raw bits go through a randomness extractor ([Extracting and testing randomness](/learn/randomness-extraction/)). Bell tests can certify new randomness from untrusted devices, given a little seed randomness and devices that cannot signal each other ([Device-independent randomness](/learn/di-randomness/)).

Here the qubit is simulated: each outcome compares a number from the browser's generator with the Born probability $\tfrac12$. It matches an ideal qubit's statistics, not its physics.

</Depth>
