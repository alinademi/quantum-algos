<Depth level="kid">
Slap your hand on a spinning coin and it shows heads or tails. Measuring a qubit is a bit like that. You get one answer, 0 or 1, never something in between.

The qubit's amplitudes set the odds. A qubit that started as 0 and then went through a Hadamard gate gives 0 half the time and 1 half the time. You can know the odds, but not which answer you will get.

Then the qubit keeps its answer. On an ideal qubit, measure it again right away and you get the same answer every time.

The coin is only a picture. A coin's odds are plain chances. A qubit's odds come from amplitudes, which can add up or cancel out before you measure. Coins never do that.
</Depth>

<Depth level="student">
A qubit's state is a|0⟩ + b|1⟩, with an amplitude for each result. Measuring it the standard way gives one result, 0 or 1. The chance of each is |amplitude|², the amplitude's size times itself. This is the Born rule.

Take a qubit in the state 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩. It gives 0 with chance 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.36 and 1 with chance 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64: 36% and 64%, which add up to 100%. A minus sign does not change the odds: −0.8 also gives 64%. It still matters elsewhere, for example when you [measure in other bases](/learn/bases/).

After an ideal measurement like this, the state jumps to the result. Get 1, and the state is now |1⟩. Measure again right away and you get 1 every time. The old amplitudes are gone, so one measurement gives you just one bit. To estimate the odds, measure many copies made the same way and count.
</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">
A pure qubit state is $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. An ideal measurement in the standard basis (repeatable, projective, two outcomes) gives outcome $j$ with probability

$$
\Pr(j) = |\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2, \qquad \Pr(0) = |\alpha|^2, \quad \Pr(1) = |\beta|^2.
$$

This is the Born rule. For $\tfrac{1+2i}{3}|0\rangle - \tfrac{2}{3}|1\rangle$ it gives $5/9$ and $4/9$. For a mixed state $\rho$ it is $\Pr(j) = \langle j|\rho|j\rangle$. A general measurement ([POVM](/learn/povm/)) can have more than two outcomes, leave a different state, or not repeat.

After outcome $j$ the state is

$$
\frac{P_j|\psi\rangle}{\lVert P_j|\psi\rangle \rVert}, \qquad P_j = |j\rangle\langle j|,
$$

which is $|j\rangle$ up to a global phase. Since $P_j^2 = P_j$, measuring again right away, with no gate in between, gives $j$ with probability 1. One measurement does not read out $\alpha$ or $\beta$: it yields one classical bit, and the state that held them is gone.

The odds depend only on $|\alpha|$ and $|\beta|$. A global phase, $e^{i\theta}|\psi\rangle$, changes no odds in any measurement. A relative phase does not show here either: $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ both give 50/50, yet a measurement in another basis tells them apart ([Measuring in other bases](/learn/bases/)).

To estimate $p = |\beta|^2$, prepare $n$ identical, independent copies and count the ones. The count $k$ is binomial with mean $np$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$, so the estimate $k/n$ has standard deviation $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$: the error falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$. Two fresh copies agree with probability $p^2 + (1-p)^2$, which is $1/2$ at $p = 1/2$. The same qubit measured twice by this ideal measurement agrees with probability 1.
</Depth>
