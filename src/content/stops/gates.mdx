<Depth level="kid">

Imagine two waves on a pond. They can meet and make a bigger wave, or cancel each other. Now imagine a device that changes their shapes according to a fixed rule. It does not throw away their combined strength: if you know the rule, you can reverse it and get the original waves back.

A qubit also carries numbers called amplitudes. A quantum gate changes those numbers without losing information. Try a gate, then run its exact reverse, and you get the original qubit back. The picture breaks because qubit amplitudes are complex numbers, not physical ripples of water; the strength here means total probability.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

An ideal [qubit](/learn/qubit/) has two amplitudes. A one-qubit gate multiplies that pair by a two-by-two matrix. X exchanges the amplitudes, H gives their sum and difference over √2, and Z changes the sign of the amplitude for 1. Every ideal gate preserves the sum of the squared sizes of the amplitudes, which is always 1.

A gate has an inverse: use the inverse after the gate and the entire state returns, not just its 0/1 odds. In the lab pick a [superposition](/learn/superposition/) and compare the amplitudes, then undo the gate. Measurement is different: it can leave a record and change the state.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A gate on $n$ qubits is a $2^n$ by $2^n$ matrix $U$ satisfying $U^\dagger U=I$. This preserves inner products and hence normalization. The inverse is $U^\dagger$. For a circuit applying $U$ then $V$, the resulting matrix is $VU$. A general physical operation can be a channel rather than a unitary, but the ideal gates here are unitaries.

</Depth>
