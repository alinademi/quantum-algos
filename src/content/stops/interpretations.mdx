<Depth level="kid">

Picture one magic trick and five friends who each explain it. The trick is the double slit. One tiny particle flies at a wall with two slits and lands as one dot. After many particles, the dots make stripes. All five friends agree on the stripes and on the odds for each dot.

They disagree about what happens in between. One says the wave is only a tool for the odds. One says every possible dot happens, each in its own branch. One says the particle always has a place and a wave steers it. One says the wave really collapses at random moments. One says the wave is your bet on what you will see.

The trick picture breaks here: nobody can check the stories against these stripes. The collapse friend is the exception. In that friend's story, a big enough object, kept isolated, would stop making stripes. Real tests so far, like one with big molecules in 2019, use far smaller things.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Start: one object flies at two slits, with equal amplitudes for the two. Standard quantum mechanics gives each dot's odds: |amplitude|². In the tool, about 80 of 100 dots land in the bright bands. With no stripes, it would be 50.

Four stories keep this rule, so their odds agree. Copenhagen-style: the wave is a tool for the odds. Many worlds: no collapse, each result in its own branch. Pilot wave: the particle always has a place, with Born-rule starting odds, steered by a wave through both slits. QBism: the wave is one agent's belief.

Collapse models, such as GRW, change the theory, so they can be tested. Each particle is hit at random, alone about once in 300 million years. Imagine a speck of 10¹⁶ particles on two paths for 1 second, far apart (much farther than 10⁻⁷ m): it is hit in 63 tries out of 100. A hit picks one path and the stripes fade. Standard quantum mechanics says they stay, if nothing records the path. Interference tests are far below this size: in 2019, molecules of over 25,000 nucleons (Fein et al.). Why one result happens: the [measurement problem](/learn/measurement-problem/).

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

The tool's wave: two Gaussian packets at $\pm D/2$, width $\sigma$, in free flight ($\hbar = m = 1$). With $\tau = t/(2\sigma^2)$, each spreads to width $\sigma\sqrt{1+\tau^2}$, and

$$
|\psi|^2 \approx \tfrac12\left(|g_L|^2 + |g_R|^2\right) + |g_L||g_R|\cos kx,
$$

with $k = D\tau/\big(2\sigma^2(1+\tau^2)\big)$. Every story gives $p(x) = |\psi(x)|^2$ for this one-particle wave (GRW too: one particle almost never gets a hit).

Not everything (Bell: the wave function is not everything, or not right). Copenhagen-style views and QBism treat the state as a tool for the odds, not a picture of a physical thing. Pilot-wave theory adds positions with $\dot x = \hbar\,\mathrm{Im}(\psi'/\psi)/m$. Then $\partial_t|\psi|^2 + \partial_x(|\psi|^2\dot x) = 0$, so starting places with $|\psi|^2$ odds keep them (equivariance). That starting rule is quantum equilibrium; without it, the dots need not follow the Born rule. In one dimension, the path from quantile $u$ stays at quantile $u$. The theory is nonlocal, as the [Bell test](/learn/bell-test/) shows it must be: one particle's speed depends at once on where the others are. It still sends no signal.

Not right. GRW is a testable change to the theory. It multiplies one particle's wave by a Gaussian of width $r_C = 10^{-7}$ m at rate $\lambda = 10^{-16}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ (Stanford Encyclopedia). For $N$ particles on two paths a distance $D$ apart, each hit, averaged over where it lands, scales the coherence by the overlap of two shifted Gaussians, so the stripes keep visibility $\exp\!\big[-N\lambda t\,(1 - e^{-D^2/(4r_C^2)})\big]$. Only for $D \gg r_C$ is this $e^{-N\lambda t}$, where a hit picks a path. Standard quantum mechanics gives 1. For $N = 10^{16}$, $t = 1$ s: $D = r_C$ gives $\approx 80\%$, and $D \gg r_C$ gives $e^{-1} \approx 37\%$.

Neither. Many worlds keeps unitary change only, with branches weighted by $|\psi|^2$, and gives up one result per run. Everettians derive why the weight acts as a chance; critics doubt it.

Every story but GRW matches standard quantum mechanics (pilot wave in quantum equilibrium). Fein et al. (2019) saw interference with molecules beyond 25 kDa, about 25,000 nucleons: far too small to test GRW's rate, and later experiments have gone further, so check current results. Heating and X-ray emission tests rule out rates above about $10^{-13}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ at $r_C = 10^{-7}$ m, but not GRW's original one (Stanford Encyclopedia, "Collapse Theories", section 7, revised 2025). Related: [decoherence](/learn/decoherence/).

</Depth>
