description: "Entanglement entropy counts, in bits, how much one half of a pure pair is tied to the other half. A product pair scores 0, a Bell pair scores 1."
answer: "Entanglement entropy is the entropy of one half of a pair whose joint state is pure. It is 0 for a product state and 1 bit, called one ebit, for a Bell pair."
myth:
  claim: "Entropy means disorder, so an entangled pair must be in a messy, unknown state."
  truth: "A pair in one exact pure state has entropy 0 as a whole. The entropy belongs to one half alone, and it is above 0 because the information sits in the link between the halves."

<Depth level="kid">

Think of a two-piece puzzle. Together, the pieces make a clear picture. One piece alone tells you little.

Entanglement entropy counts how little one half tells you, in bits. One bit is the surprise of a fair coin flip.

Start two qubits as 00, then use a Hadamard gate and a CNOT to make a Bell pair. The pair is in one exact state. Yet one qubit alone gives a fair coin flip in any basis: 1 bit, called one ebit. If each qubit has a pure state of its own, the count is 0 bits: measure one the right way and nothing surprises you. A gate on one qubit alone does not change this count.

The picture breaks here: a puzzle piece has its picture all along. In standard quantum mechanics, one half of an entangled pair has no pure state, only odds: its state is mixed. Only the pair is in a pure state. And this count needs the whole pair to be in a pure state.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Start as 00, then a Hadamard gate on the first qubit and a [CNOT](/learn/cnot/): the Bell pair (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. The pair is one exact state, with entropy 0. The pair has equal amplitudes for 00 and 11, so measuring A alone gives 0 or 1 with equal chance: a fair coin.

Entropy scores odds: add p × log2(1/p) over the odds, in bits. A fair coin gives ½ + ½ = 1 bit. Odds of 90% and 10% give about 0.47.

Write a pure pair in [Schmidt form](/learn/schmidt/): terms with weights adding to 1. The entanglement entropy is the entropy of the weights, the same for A and B. A product has one weight, 100%: entropy 0. Two qubits reach at most 1 bit, from weights of 50% and 50%. A gate on one qubit alone leaves the weights alone. Measuring A in any basis (a test with two results) gives an average surprise per shot never below this entropy; the basis of A's Schmidt terms reaches it.

This needs the pair to be pure: a coin that picks 00 or 11 also gives A 1 bit, with no entanglement.

One ebit is one Bell pair's worth. Take a large number n of copies of the same pure pair: local actions plus messages turn them into about n times the entanglement entropy (in bits) Bell pairs, and back.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A pure state has Schmidt form $|\psi\rangle = \sum_i \sqrt{\lambda_i}\,|a_i\rangle|b_i\rangle$ with $\lambda_i > 0$, $\sum_i \lambda_i = 1$ ([Schmidt](/learn/schmidt/)). Then $\rho_A = \sum_i \lambda_i |a_i\rangle\langle a_i|$, and $\rho_B$ has the same eigenvalues. So

$$E(\psi) = S(\rho_A) = S(\rho_B) = -\sum_i \lambda_i \log_2 \lambda_i,$$

while $S(\rho_{AB}) = 0$ (Wilde, Theorem 11.2.1). Here $S$ is the information missing about one half, in bits. It equals a thermodynamic entropy (times $k_B \ln 2$) only for special states, such as thermal ones. $E = 0$ exactly for products. $E \le \log_2 \min(d_A, d_B)$, with equality exactly when there are $\min(d_A, d_B)$ weights, all equal. $U_A \otimes U_B$ keeps $E$, and $E(\psi_{AB} \otimes \phi_{A'B'}) = E(\psi) + E(\phi)$ across the cut $AA'|BB'$.

For two qubits, $\det \rho_A = |\psi_{00}\psi_{11} - \psi_{01}\psi_{10}|^2$, the quantity from [entanglement](/learn/entanglement/). With $C = 2\,|\psi_{00}\psi_{11} - \psi_{01}\psi_{10}|$,

$$\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12\bigl(1 \pm \sqrt{1 - C^2}\bigr).$$

Started as $|00\rangle$, a gate on A that makes $\sqrt{1-p}\,|0\rangle + \sqrt{p}\,|1\rangle$, then a CNOT, give $\sqrt{1-p}\,|00\rangle + \sqrt{p}\,|11\rangle$, so $E = h(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)$.

Also $S(\rho) = \min_U H(\operatorname{diag} U\rho U^\dagger)$ (Aaronson, eq. 11.6): measuring A in any orthonormal basis gives results with Shannon entropy at least $E$, equal in the eigenbasis of $\rho_A$. Every rank-one POVM also gives at least $E$; only a coarse-grained one goes lower, such as a one-outcome test, with entropy 0.

For $n$ copies, local operations and classical communication convert $|\psi\rangle^{\otimes n}$ to about $nE$ Bell pairs and back as $n$ grows (Wilde, ch. 19). A single copy cannot always be converted exactly.

This fails for mixed pairs: $\tfrac12(|00\rangle\langle 00| + |11\rangle\langle 11|)$ has $S(\rho_A) = 1$, yet it is a mix of products.

</Depth>
