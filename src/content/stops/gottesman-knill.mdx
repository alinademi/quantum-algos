<Depth level="kid">

Imagine a long row of light switches. Instead of writing down every possible row, you write rules like "this light and that light always agree". A small list of rules can tell you a great deal about a very large row. Now suppose you can rearrange the switches in ways that turn each rule into another rule of the same kind. You never need the giant list.

Some quantum experiments work like that. A normal computer can predict their answers quickly even when the qubits are linked. The picture breaks because the real rules describe quantum measurements, not hidden values of every switch. Other quantum operations break the useful pattern.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A circuit made of Clifford gates, starting in a [stabilizer state](/learn/stabilizer-states/), can be sampled efficiently on a normal computer. This is the Gottesman–Knill theorem. Update the short list of signed Pauli rules after each gate. To measure an observable Q, check whether +Q or −Q is a rule obtained by multiplying the generators. The respective expectations are +1 and −1: the answer is certain. Otherwise the expectation is zero, giving each sign with equal chance.

The lab computes these odds without allocating a state vector, even for 64 qubits. Adding T supplies a universal set, and the theorem no longer guarantees efficient simulation.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A pure stabilizer state has $n$ independent signed Pauli generators, whose products form $S$. Represent them by binary X and Z components and signs. Clifford conjugation updates these in polynomial time, and Pauli measurements update the generators and can feed classical results forward. For Hermitian Pauli Q, the expectation is $+1$ if $Q\in S$, $-1$ if $-Q\in S$, and zero otherwise. The odds of $+1$ and $-1$ are $(1+E)/2$ and $(1-E)/2$.

The lab uses binary elimination with sign tracking to test group membership before a single selected measurement. It illustrates the [classical simulation](/learn/classical-simulation/) theorem, not a full dynamic simulator. Adding T to [Clifford gates](/learn/clifford-group/) gives an approximately [universal set](/learn/universal/); Gottesman–Knill no longer covers arbitrary such circuits (some remain easy). See [binary arithmetic](/learn/gf2/) and [Aaronson and Gottesman](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0406196).

</Depth>
