<Depth level="kid">

Light comes in tiny bits called photons. Send one photon into a special box. Inside, a half mirror gives it two ways to go. Two plain mirrors bring the two ways together again at a second half mirror. Then the photon comes out at one of two doors, D0 or D1.

You might guess each door gets half the photons. But every photon comes out at D0. Each way carries a wave-like number called an amplitude. At D1 the two amplitudes cancel, like a high wave meeting a low wave. At D0 they add up.

Now put a marker in the box that keeps a record of which way each photon went. Nobody has to read it. The canceling stops, and each door gets half.

Where the wave picture breaks: a wave spreads out, but each photon comes out at just one door, as one whole dot.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

The photon starts in the upper input. Here we take each beam splitter as a [Hadamard gate](/learn/hadamard/), $H$. Many optics books put an $i$ on reflection instead. Then, in this picture, D0 is the dark one at 0°. Which detector is dark depends on the parts. That one stays dark does not. The first beam splitter acts like a Hadamard gate: each arm gets amplitude $1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.71$.

The second beam splitter splits each arm again. There are two ways to reach D0, each with amplitude $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \times \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \tfrac{1}{2}$. They add: $\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$. Two ways reach D1 too, but one picks up a minus sign: $\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} = 0$. Probability is $|\text{amplitude}|^2$, so D0 gets 100% and D1 gets 0%. It is the same sum as in [interference](/learn/interference/).

A phase shifter, such as a thin glass plate, delays the lower arm and changes its phase. At 180° it flips that arm's sign, so now the ways to D0 cancel and every photon goes to D1.

Now let a marker record each photon's arm. The two ways to D1 end with records that tell them apart for sure, so they cannot cancel. Each detector gets 50%, at any phase.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Treat the path as a qubit: $|0\rangle$ is the upper arm, $|1\rangle$ the lower arm. A balanced beam splitter acts as the Hadamard gate $H$ (Zwiebach, eq. 1.14), and a phase shifter in the lower arm is $P(\varphi) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\varphi})$. The photon starts in $|0\rangle$, and D0 reads the $|0\rangle$ output:

$$
H\,P(\varphi)\,H\,|0\rangle = \frac{1+e^{i\varphi}}{2}\,|0\rangle + \frac{1-e^{i\varphi}}{2}\,|1\rangle,
\qquad p(D_0) = \cos^2\tfrac{\varphi}{2}, \quad p(D_1) = \sin^2\tfrac{\varphi}{2}.
$$

At $\varphi = 0$, $HH = I$ and D1 never clicks. A phase on both arms is a global phase and changes nothing; only the relative phase $\varphi$ moves the odds.

To mark the path, let a marker end in $|m_0\rangle$ or $|m_1\rangle$, depending on the arm. Before the second beam splitter the state is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|0\rangle|m_0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle|m_1\rangle\big)$, so

$$
p(D_0) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(1 + \mathrm{Re}\big(e^{i\varphi}\langle m_0|m_1\rangle\big)\Big).
$$

A full record, $\langle m_0|m_1\rangle = 0$, gives $\tfrac{1}{2}$ at every $\varphi$. A partial record leaves fringes of visibility $|\langle m_0|m_1\rangle|$. Nobody has to read the marker.

Real beam splitters can add fixed phases at their ports. Any balanced (50/50), lossless two-port splitter is $H$ with phases before and after. So the fringe only shifts by a constant: at $\varphi = 0$ the dark port may move, or neither port may be dark. Block one arm instead, and the photon is absorbed half the time and reaches each detector a quarter of the time, so D1 now clicks. That is the start of the [bomb tester](/learn/bomb-tester/).

</Depth>
