<Depth level="kid">

Imagine writing a list of all possible patterns of light switches. One switch has two patterns, two have four and three have eight. Every extra switch doubles the list. A general quantum state attaches a complex number to each pattern, so a complete list rapidly becomes too big for an ordinary computer. Sometimes a short rule describes the whole list, like saying every entry is the same, or saying only the all-off and all-on entries are nonzero. Other patterns cannot be compressed so easily. The picture breaks because the numbers in the quantum list are wave amplitudes, not independent probabilities, and a simulator must also preserve their phases.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A dense state vector for n [qubits](/learn/multi-qubit/) stores 2ⁿ complex amplitudes. At 16 bytes each, 30 qubits need 16 GiB before overhead, and every added qubit doubles the cost. But a [tensor product](/learn/tensor-product/) of n single-qubit states needs only n small descriptions. A [stabilizer state](/learn/stabilizer-states/) has n signed Pauli rules. Tensor networks can represent states with restricted [entanglement](/learn/entanglement/) efficiently, depending on bond dimension and circuit structure. GHZ has only two nonzero amplitudes despite being entangled. Its matrix product representation has two internal values (bond dimension 2), while the product state needs only one. Crossed Bell pairs need a bond dimension that grows exponentially across the middle cut, despite having simple stabilizer rules. Compare the memory costs in the lab. A classical simulator's resource cost depends on which representation and task it uses.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A generic pure-state vector requires exponentially many parameters; a dense density matrix has $4^n$ complex entries. Stabilizer tableaux use polynomial space. An open-boundary matrix product state with uniform bond dimension D uses $O(nD^2)$ complex slots for qubits. A product state has D=1; GHZ has D=2 across every nontrivial cut since the two terms have orthogonal factors on each side. For n even, Bell pairs joining wire i to i+n/2 have cut rank $2^{\min(k,n-k)}$ at cut k in that fixed ordering. Permuting wires can change the ranks. A product of T|+⟩ states has D=1 but is not a stabilizer state. These are representation-dependent bounds, not a universal limit on exact or approximate simulation of a particular circuit. A state-vector simulator and a sampling algorithm need not store the same representation.

Dense storage illustrates the exponential growth in [powers and logs](/learn/powers-and-logs/); it need not be the cost of simulating a particular [circuit](/learn/circuits/).

</Depth>
