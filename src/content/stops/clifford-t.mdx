<Depth level="kid">

Imagine drawing shapes with a set of tools that only make quarter turns, together with a new tool that makes an eighth of a turn. Two new turns can be replaced by a single old turn, four make a half turn and eight make a full turn. But used among other tools, the new turn opens a much larger range of possible shapes. Quantum computers can use this extra type of turn to approximate any allowed operation, with suitable connecting tools. Counting the new turns is useful when they are expensive to perform. The drawing picture breaks because the angles describe relative signs and complex amplitudes rather than ordinary lines.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

The T gate is a [phase gate](/learn/phase-gates/) of 45°. Two T gates give S, four give Z, and eight give I; T† reverses T. Combining [Clifford gates](/learn/clifford-group/) and T gives an approximately [universal gate set](/learn/universal/), able to approximate any finite-qubit unitary up to global phase. In many fault-tolerant designs T gates need costly resources, so engineers count T and T† separately from Clifford operations. They also count T depth, the number of sequential layers of T gates. Raw T symbols may overstate cost: adjacent powers can be merged into Clifford gates, sometimes removing every T. The lab compares raw and simplified counts.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

T is $\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\pi/4})$ and is non-Clifford: $TXT^\dagger=(X+Y)/\sqrt2$. For fixed n, the Clifford group modulo global phase is finite and its stabilizer circuits are efficiently simulated by [Gottesman-Knill](/learn/gottesman-knill/). Adding T gives access to rotations about distinct axes by angles that are irrational multiples of $2\pi$: for example, the Bloch rotation of THTH has half-angle cosine $\cos^2(\pi/8)$. Together these rotations give a dense single-qubit set ([Boykin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9906054)); adding CNOT gives universality up to global phase. [Solovay-Kitaev](/learn/solovay-kitaev/) explains approximation. For fixed precision, [Bravyi-Gosset](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.07601) gives simulation algorithms polynomial in circuit size and exponential in T count. Fault-tolerant resource estimates often count T gates and T depth because a logical T can require expensive magic-state resources in common error-correcting architectures. These are architecture-dependent costs, and syntactic T count may overestimate a simplified circuit.

</Depth>
