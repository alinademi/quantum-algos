<Depth level="kid">

Imagine a set of tracks with instructions printed from left to right. An object on a track passes each instruction in order. Some instructions act on a single track; others link two tracks. A meter at the end gives a recorded answer. If two instructions use the same track, changing their order can change the answer, just as folding a piece of paper and then cutting it is different from cutting and then folding.

Quantum circuits draw qubits as tracks. The picture breaks because no little object is physically travelling along the printed wire. The wire shows which part of a joint quantum state each instruction acts on.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Read a quantum circuit from left to right. Each wire is a qubit. A box represents a gate applied to that wire, and a connected control dot and target plus sign represent [CNOT](/learn/cnot/). A meter marks a measurement, whether at the end or during the circuit.

Start with 00. H on q0 then CNOT makes a Bell pair; reversing the order leaves |+⟩|0⟩. In matrix notation the rightmost operation acts first: H then CNOT is CNOT·(H⊗I). The lab shows the matrix on a gate’s own wires and the full two-wire circuit matrix separately. A one-wire matrix is padded with identity on the untouched wire. Gates on disjoint wires commute and can often be executed in the same layer.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

With column vectors, chronological gates $U_1,\ldots,U_m$ give $U_m\cdots U_1|\psi\rangle$. Operations on disjoint subsystems commute, but overlapping ones need not. The lab uses the convention $|q_0q_1\rangle$, with q0 the most significant displayed bit. Measurement and classical control require more than a single unitary matrix.

For example the whole-circuit matrix for [Hadamard](/learn/hadamard/) on q0 followed by CNOT is $CX_{01}(H\otimes I)$. A meter signifies a computational-basis measurement after that matrix acts; its outcomes obey the Born rule.

</Depth>
