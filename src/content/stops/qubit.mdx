<Depth level="kid">

A bit is like a light switch: off or on, 0 or 1. Check it as often as you like, and it stays the same.

A qubit is a tiny thing with two states, which we call 0 and 1. It could be an atom in a low or a high energy state. Read a qubit, and you also get just 0 or 1.

Here is the twist. A qubit can take a half flip. Start at 0 and do it once: the reading is 0 or 1 at random, like a coin toss. Do it twice, with no reading in between: you get 1 every time. A coin tossed twice is still random. So the coin picture breaks here: a qubit is more than a coin hiding 0 or 1.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A bit is 0 or 1. A qubit is a quantum system with two levels, written |0⟩ and |1⟩ (say "ket 0" and "ket 1"). It can be an electron's spin, up or down, a photon's polarization, or two energy levels of an atom.

A qubit in |0⟩ reads 0 every time, and one in |1⟩ reads 1. A flip (the NOT gate) swaps them. So far, just like a bit.

But a qubit's state is set by two amplitudes, one for 0 and one for 1. The chance of reading 0 is |amplitude for 0|². A reading still gives one bit.

Amplitudes allow a half flip. Started as 0, one half flip gives 0 or 1 with 50% each; two in a row, with no reading between, give 1 every time. On a bit, the only step with 50/50 odds from both 0 and 1 is a coin toss, and two tosses still give 50/50. More at [Superposition](/learn/superposition/).

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A qubit is a two-level quantum system. Its state is a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
$$

The basis states $|0\rangle = (1, 0)^\mathsf{T}$ and $|1\rangle = (0, 1)^\mathsf{T}$ stand for the bit values. Physical qubits include an electron's spin, a photon's polarization, and two levels picked from an atom's many.

A random bit is a probability vector $(p_0, p_1)$, and its steps are stochastic matrices. A qubit has complex amplitudes instead, and its steps are unitary matrices. Measuring in the standard basis gives 0 with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and 1 with probability $|\beta|^2$ (Born rule). No measurement of one qubit yields more than one bit of information about how it was prepared ([Holevo bound](/learn/holevo-bound/)), if no entanglement was shared beforehand; with a shared Bell pair, one sent qubit carries two bits ([superdense coding](/learn/superdense/)). A global phase, $|\psi\rangle \to e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$, cannot be seen; the relative phase of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can.

The gap shows in a square root of NOT, often written $\sqrt{X}$ ($H$ the Hadamard gate, $S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i)$):

$$
R = HSH = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1+i & 1-i \\ 1-i & 1+i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R^2 = X
$$

$R|0\rangle$ and $R|1\rangle$ each read 0 or 1 with probability $\tfrac12$. The only stochastic matrix that sends both 0 and 1 to $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ is $C = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $C^2 = C$. In fact no stochastic $M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 1-a & 1-b \end{pmatrix}$ has $M^2 = X$: $(M^2)_{00} = a^2 + b(1-a)$ is zero only when $a = b = 0$, and then $M^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

Bits can also be copied; an unknown qubit state cannot ([no-cloning](/learn/no-cloning/)).

</Depth>
