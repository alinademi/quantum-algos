<Depth level="kid">

Picture a game with two rules. Rule one: pieces glide along on their own. Rule two: when a player checks a piece, roll a die to place it. The rule book never says what counts as checking. A camera? A dog?

Quantum physics has two rules like that. Gates change a qubit's amplitudes smoothly, with no dice. A measurement gives one random answer. The rules never say what counts.

The picture breaks here: in a game, players are not pieces. But a measuring machine is made of atoms, like the qubit. A qubit started as 0, then a Hadamard gate: equal amplitudes for 0 and 1. Apply rule one to the machine too, and the math gives a superposition of "shows 0" and "shows 1". Yet we always see one answer.

Air bumping the machine hides the clues of that superposition, but picks no answer. What really happens is still open.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Standard quantum mechanics has two rules for change. Gates change amplitudes smoothly, with no randomness, and can be undone. A measurement gives one random result, with probability |amplitude|², and the state jumps to it: a collapse. The rules never say which processes count.

Start a qubit as 0, then a Hadamard gate: equal amplitudes, 0.71 each, for 0 and 1. A meter is made of atoms too. Let it record the qubit by the gate rule, and you get 0.71|0, reads 0⟩ + 0.71|1, reads 1⟩, not one reading. Reading the meter gives 0 or 1 with 50% each, collapse or not. Only gates that make the readings interfere, then a measurement, can tell.

Air molecules bump a real meter, each keeping a tiny record: [decoherence](/learn/decoherence/). After many, tests on the qubit and meter alone can barely tell collapse from no collapse, yet no reading is picked. Why we see one result is open; [interpretations](/learn/interpretations/) differ.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Standard quantum mechanics has two kinds of change: $|\psi\rangle \to U|\psi\rangle$, linear and reversible, and measurement, which gives outcome $j$ with probability $|\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2$ and collapses the state. It does not say which processes are measurements.

Give the meter a ready state $|R\rangle$ that works: $|j\rangle|R\rangle \to |j\rangle|R_j\rangle$. Linearity then forces

$$
(a|0\rangle + b|1\rangle)|R\rangle \to a|0\rangle|R_0\rangle + b|1\rangle|R_1\rangle,
$$

an entangled state with no definite reading. (With a person as meter: Wigner's friend.) Reading the meter gives $|a|^2$ and $|b|^2$ with or without a collapse first. Only an interference test across the readings tells them apart.

Let $n$ molecules keep weak records: $a|0\rangle|R_0\rangle|E_0\rangle + b|1\rangle|R_1\rangle|E_1\rangle$. Tracing out the air multiplies the cross terms by $\langle E_1|E_0\rangle$ or its conjugate. A collapse sets them to 0. The [trace distance](/learn/distance-measures/) between those two reduced states is $|ab\,\langle E_0|E_1\rangle|$, so no test on qubit and meter alone moves a chance by more. In the lab below, $|a| = |b| = 1/\sqrt2$ and $\langle E_0|E_1\rangle = 0.9^n$.

This is decoherence. It explains why tests on the qubit and meter alone show almost no interference between readings. It gives no single outcome: it is the unitary rule at work, so the total state is still a superposition. The reduced state is an improper mixture, from entanglement, so it does not by itself say a result happened.

Bell (1987): "Either the wavefunction, as given by the Schrödinger equation, is not everything, or it is not right." Pilot-wave theory adds particle positions; some Copenhagen variants deny the state represents reality: not everything. Collapse models such as GRW change the dynamics: not right. Many-worlds rejects both horns: the state is complete and always unitary. See [Interpretations](/learn/interpretations/).

</Depth>
