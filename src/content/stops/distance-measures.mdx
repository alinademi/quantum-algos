<Depth level="kid">

Two machines each print a dot, red or blue, with set chances. A friend picks one machine in secret, each equally often, and hands you one dot. You guess which machine made it.

If the machines print alike, you are right about half the time. If one prints only red and the other only blue, you are right every time. The trace distance is a score from 0 to 1 for how far above a coin flip your best guess can get: your best chance of being right is one half plus half the score.

Two quantum states are like two such machines, except that each way of measuring gives its own set of chances. The trace distance counts the best way.

Fidelity is a second score, for overlap: 1 for the same state, 0 for states that one measurement tells apart every time.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Measure a qubit and each result has a chance. Two states can give different chances. The trace distance T is the biggest gap in the chance of one result that any way of measuring can make: 0 for the same state, 1 when a measurement tells the two apart every time.

Say A started as 0, and B started as 0, then a Hadamard gate. Measured as 0 or 1, A gives 0 every time and B gives 0 with chance 50%: a gap of 0.50. Tilting the measurement toward |−⟩ widens the gap, up to T = 0.71 at 45°. No measurement goes past it. If a fair coin picks A or B, the best guess is right with chance ½ + ½T, about 85%.

Fidelity F scores overlap: 1 for the same state, 0 for states a measurement tells apart every time. For pure states, F = |⟨ψ|φ⟩|, here 0.71. If A is pure, F² is the chance that B passes the test "is it A?", 50% here.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

For two states $\rho$ and $\sigma$, the trace distance and the fidelity are

$$
T = \tfrac12\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_1, \qquad F = \operatorname{tr}\sqrt{\sqrt\rho\,\sigma\sqrt\rho} = \lVert\sqrt\rho\,\sqrt\sigma\rVert_1.
$$

Some authors, Wilde among them, call $F^2$ the fidelity; there Fuchs-van de Graaf reads $1-\sqrt F\le T\le\sqrt{1-F}$. For the norms, see [matrix norms](/learn/matrix-norms/).

$T$ is the largest gap in the chance of one outcome, $T = \max_{0\le E\le I}\operatorname{tr}[E(\rho-\sigma)]$. With equal priors, the best one-shot guess is right with chance $\tfrac12+\tfrac12T$ (Helstrom, see [telling states apart](/learn/state-discrimination/)). $T=0$ only for equal states, $T=1$ only when the supports are orthogonal, and unitaries keep $T$, and no channel can raise it.

$F$ is symmetric, $F=1$ only for equal states, $F=0$ only for orthogonal supports, and no channel can lower it. For pure states $F=|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|$, and if $\psi$ is pure, $F^2=\langle\psi|\sigma|\psi\rangle$. Uhlmann's theorem: $F$ is the largest $|\langle\Psi_\rho|\Psi_\sigma\rangle|$ over [purifications](/learn/purification/) of $\rho$ and $\sigma$.

The Fuchs-van de Graaf inequalities tie them:

$$
1 - F \le T \le \sqrt{1-F^2}.
$$

The right side is equality for pure pairs. Mixed pairs can sit strictly inside, so $F$ does not fix $T$.

For a qubit, $\rho=\tfrac12(I+\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ and $\sigma=\tfrac12(I+\mathbf s\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ give

$$
T=\tfrac12|\mathbf r-\mathbf s|, \qquad F^2=\tfrac12\Big(1+\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s+\sqrt{(1-r^2)(1-s^2)}\Big).
$$

The second follows because $\operatorname{tr}\sqrt M=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}M+2\sqrt{\det M}}$ for a $2\times2$ positive $M$. For pure states at angle $\Delta$ on the Bloch sphere, $F=\cos\tfrac\Delta2$ and $T=\sin\tfrac\Delta2$.

</Depth>
