<Depth level="kid">
A measurement is a question with a fixed list of answers. Picture a dial that only clicks to a few marks, never in between.

An observable is a quantity you can measure, like the spin of an atom along one direction. Its marks are its possible results.

For a qubit, quantum theory writes each observable as a small grid of numbers, called a matrix. Its results are special numbers worked out from the grid, called eigenvalues. They are not just the numbers on the grid's diagonal: in the lab below, the diagonal holds 1 and −2, yet every result is 2 or −3.

Each result has its own state. A qubit in that state gives that result every time. Other states give one of the results at random.

Where the dial picture breaks: a real dial just reads a thing. Measuring a qubit can change it. After a clean measurement, it sits in the state for the result it gave.
</Depth>

<Depth level="student">
An observable is a quantity you can measure, written as a Hermitian matrix $A$, one equal to its own conjugate transpose. The rules:

- The results are the eigenvalues of $A$: the numbers $\lambda$ with $Av = \lambda v$ for an eigenvector $v$. They are always real.
- A result's chance is |amplitude|², where the amplitude is the state's part along that result's eigenvector. The eigenvectors are at right angles, so the chances add up to 100%.
- After an ideal measurement, the state is that eigenvector (when no two eigenvalues are equal), so measuring $A$ again right away gives the same result.

$Z$, with rows (1, 0) and (0, −1), is the usual measurement with new names for the results: |0⟩ has eigenvalue 1 and |1⟩ has −1.

Now take $A$ with rows (1, 2) and (2, −2). Its eigenvalues are 2 and −3, with eigenvectors (2, 1)/√5 and (1, −2)/√5. Started as 0, the qubit has amplitude 2/√5 along the first and 1/√5 along the second. So it gives 2 with chance 4/5 and −3 with chance 1/5. Never 1 or −2.
</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">
An observable is a Hermitian operator, $A = A^\dagger$. By the [spectral theorem](/learn/spectral-theorem/),

$$
A = \sum_k \lambda_k \Pi_k, \qquad \sum_k \Pi_k = I,
$$

with distinct real eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ and orthogonal projectors $\Pi_k$ onto the eigenspaces. An ideal (Lüders) measurement of $A$ is the projective measurement $\{\Pi_k\}$ with outcomes labeled $\lambda_k$. Outcome $\lambda_k$ has probability $p_k = \langle\psi|\Pi_k|\psi\rangle$, which is $|\langle v_k|\psi\rangle|^2$ when $\Pi_k = |v_k\rangle\langle v_k|$, and leaves the state

$$
\frac{\Pi_k|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{p_k}}.
$$

Hermiticity makes this a measurement: real eigenvalues, and an orthonormal eigenbasis, so the $p_k$ sum to 1. The non-Hermitian matrix with rows $(1, 1)$ and $(0, 2)$ has eigenvectors $|0\rangle$ and $|{+}\rangle$, not at right angles; for $|0\rangle$ the would-be odds $|\langle v_k|0\rangle|^2$ sum to $3/2$, not 1.

The eigenvalues are labels; the projectors are the measurement. $Z$, $|1\rangle\langle 1| = (I - Z)/2$ and the spin-½ observable $S_z = \tfrac{\hbar}{2} Z$ are one measurement: $|0\rangle$ reads $1$, $0$ or $+\hbar/2$, and $|1\rangle$ reads $-1$, $1$ or $-\hbar/2$. A repeated eigenvalue has a projector of rank above 1, and the state lands at $\Pi_k|\psi\rangle/\sqrt{p_k}$ in that eigenspace, not at an eigenvector fixed in advance: $Z \otimes Z$ reads only the parity of two qubits, so $a|00\rangle + b|11\rangle$ reads $+1$ and is left unchanged, coherence kept. A real instrument can disturb the state differently; this update is the ideal one.

Every one-qubit observable is $a_0 I + \vec a \cdot \vec\sigma$, with $\vec\sigma = (X, Y, Z)$ and real $a_0$, $\vec a$. For $\vec a \neq 0$ its eigenvalues are $a_0 \pm |\vec a|$, with eigenvectors at the Bloch points $\pm\hat a$: measuring it is [measuring in the basis](/learn/bases/) along $\hat a$, then relabeling. The lab's Surprise matrix has $a_0 = -\tfrac12$ and $\vec a = (2, 0, \tfrac32)$, so $\lambda = -\tfrac12 \pm \tfrac52$.

$A|0\rangle = (1, 2)^T$ is no result, not even a unit vector. But $\langle 0|A|0\rangle = 1 = \tfrac45 \cdot 2 + \tfrac15 \cdot (-3)$ is the [average result](/learn/expectation-values/).
</Depth>
