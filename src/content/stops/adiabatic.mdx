<Depth level="kid">

Imagine a ball in a bowl whose lowest point moves slowly from one side to another. If you move the bowl gently, the ball can stay near its lowest point. A quantum experiment can similarly begin in the lowest-energy state of an easy system and slowly change the energy rule to encode an answer. The smaller the separation from the next energy level, the more carefully you generally need to move. A rapidly shrinking separation can make the process very slow. Sometimes the ball returns near the lowest point at the end despite wandering earlier. The picture breaks because an isolated quantum system evolves by amplitudes rather than friction, and a slow change is not a universal shortcut.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Start in |+⟩, the ground state of −X, and [evolve](/learn/time-evolution/) toward the ground state |0⟩ of −Z. Their interpolated Hamiltonian has a minimum gap of √2 in the chosen units. A common adiabatic time-scale estimate is change-rate scale divided by the squared minimum gap: smaller gaps may require much longer runtime, potentially exponential. Examine the probability throughout the path, not only its final value: at time 5 our example nearly reaches 1 at the end despite a dip earlier. General adiabatic computation is polynomially equivalent to [universal circuits](/learn/universal/), but restricted stoquastic [annealing](/learn/annealing/) is not known to be universal. This simulator is an ideal one-qubit numerical example.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

With $\hbar=1$, $H(s)=-(1-s)X-sZ$ and $s=t/T$. The spectral gap is $2\sqrt{(1-s)^2+s^2}$, minimum √2 at s=1/2. A rough adiabatic scale for smooth gapped paths is $T\gg\|\partial_s H\|/\Delta_{\min}^2$; rigorous bounds also involve path derivatives and transition matrix elements. An exponentially small gap can lead to an exponentially long runtime under such schedules. This lab uses 1000 midpoint constant-H exponentials, approximating time-ordered evolution. Final success need not increase monotonically with T: finite-time interference can produce a nearly perfect final overlap even after an earlier dip. General adiabatic models are polynomially equivalent to circuits ([Aharonov et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0405098)); restricted stoquastic annealing, including this simple minus-X/minus-Z example, is not known to be universal. No optimization speedup is implied.

The ideas used here build on [Simulating physics](/learn/simulation/).

</Depth>
