<Depth level="kid">

Imagine two wave shapes entering a device that compares leaving them in place with exchanging them. If they have exactly the same shape, exchanging them makes no difference. Their contributions then reinforce perfectly at one exit. If they are completely different in the special sense used by this test, the two exits are equally likely. Other pairs fall between those cases. A single result does not tell you how similar the shapes are: even completely different ones can exit the first way. You need fresh copies of both for each trial. The picture breaks because this test compares complex quantum amplitudes rather than visible wave shapes.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Prepare a helper in |0⟩, apply [H](/learn/hadamard/), perform a controlled [SWAP](/learn/fredkin/) of two independent pure states, then H and measure the helper. If they are identical up to global phase, the helper is always 0. If their [inner product](/learn/inner-product/) is zero, both helper outcomes occur with equal probability. In general twice the fraction of zeros minus one estimates the squared size of their inner product. A 0 result alone proves nothing about equality. Every shot requires a fresh pair of input states and a fresh helper; the test measures the helper, not the individual state descriptions. Try parallel, perpendicular and intermediate Bloch directions.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

For input $|\psi\rangle|\phi\rangle$, the symmetric and antisymmetric components have weights $p(0)=(1+|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^2)/2$ and $p(1)=(1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^2)/2$. The helper Z expectation is the squared overlap. For independent mixed states the same test estimates $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\sigma)$, not generally their Uhlmann fidelity.

The controlled [SWAP](/learn/toffoli/) is what correlates the symmetric and antisymmetric components with the helper. Only the helper needs to be [measured](/learn/measurement/); repeated runs consume fresh input pairs.

</Depth>
