<Depth level="kid">

Imagine two instruction cards for moving a toy. One says turn left twice and then right twice. The other says do nothing. The two cards always have the same effect if those turns undo each other. You can replace one card with the other and save work.

But two different cards might happen to give the same answer for one toy position and not for another. Check every input, and for quantum instructions check the amplitudes and their phases too. The picture breaks because a real quantum circuit acts on a joint wave of possibilities, not just on a list of positions. Equal measured odds alone do not establish that two instructions are equivalent.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Circuit identities let us replace a sequence by another with the same operation. H X H equals Z. Two CNOTs on the same wires cancel. CZ is symmetric between its two wires. H on both wires before and after a CNOT reverses the direction of the control.

To prove a replacement, compare the complete matrices, not one selected input. For example Z and I agree exactly on |0⟩, but Z turns |+⟩ into |−⟩. Z and I give the same 0/1 odds on every input, yet are not the same gate. The lab compares both sequences and their maximum matrix-entry difference.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Use $HXH=Z$, $H^2=I$, $CX^2=I$. In two-qubit notation, $(H\otimes H)CX_{0,1}(H\otimes H)=CX_{1,0}$. Equality of all matrix entries establishes equality on arbitrary superpositions and entangled inputs. Equality only up to a global phase needs care if a subcircuit is controlled.

For [controlled gates](/learn/controlled-gates/), CZ is diagonal with entries $(1,1,1,-1)$, so swapping its two wires leaves its matrix unchanged. The lab compares the complete matrices using the chronological ordering described in [reading a circuit](/learn/circuits/).

</Depth>
