<Depth level="kid">

Imagine a recipe that tells you to open an envelope halfway through and use the number inside to choose the next instruction. You can continue the recipe without knowing in advance which instruction you will follow. A quantum experiment can also record an answer partway through and respond to it. The answer may come out differently on different runs. It can even tell you how to put the measured part back into a known starting state. There is an important difference from the envelope picture: reading the quantum part changes its state, and resetting it does not recover the original wave.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

[Measurement](/learn/measurement/) need not be the last operation of a [circuit](/learn/circuits/). Prepare |+⟩ on q0 and |0⟩ on q1, then CNOT. Measuring q0 gives 0 or 1 with equal probability and changes the joint state to 00 or 11 respectively. If the result is 1, X on q1 makes q1 zero in either case; instead X on q0 resets q0 to zero. Neither action restores the earlier superposition. Circuits that measure during execution and use a classical outcome to select later operations are called dynamic circuits. The lab lists the conditional states and the averaged outcome distribution.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

For projectors $P_m$, branch probability is $p_m=\langle\psi|P_m|\psi\rangle$ and conditional state is $P_m|\psi\rangle/\sqrt{p_m}$ for $p_m>0$. Outcome-dependent gates $U_m$ produce the ensemble $\sum_m U_mP_m\rho P_mU_m^\dagger$. Resetting a measured bit by conditional X is not a unitary inverse.

</Depth>
