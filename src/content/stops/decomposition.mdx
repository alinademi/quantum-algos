<Depth level="kid">

Imagine a huge machine whose effect is described by a table. To take it apart, use small adjustments that change just two entries of the output description at a time, until the main table is simple. Reverse those adjustments and you have rebuilt the original machine. Each small adjustment can itself be broken into basic instructions. The longer the table, the more independent details it may have. Adding extra quantum wires makes the table grow rapidly, so almost every general transformation needs an enormous number of basic instructions. The picture breaks because the table contains complex amplitudes and the adjustments must preserve total probability and be reversible.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A two-level unitary changes just two computational-basis amplitudes. Use such rotations to eliminate entries below the matrix diagonal while preserving unitarity. The remaining unitary is diagonal; reverse the elimination to rebuild the target. Each two-level factor can be reduced to [multi-controlled gates](/learn/multi-controlled/) by moving the two affected basis labels until they differ in one bit. Those gates decompose into [one-qubit gates](/learn/single-qubit-unitary/) and CNOTs. This proves exact [universality](/learn/universal/), but not short circuits for every target: almost every n-qubit unitary needs exponentially many gates because it has exponentially many independent parameters. This route is constructive, not short. A different method gives any two-qubit gate with at most three CNOTs plus local gates ([two-qubit synthesis](/learn/two-qubit-synthesis/)). Compare both counts in the lab.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Complex Givens rotations mix rows of a unitary U to produce an upper-triangular unitary, necessarily diagonal. If $G_m\cdots G_1 U=D$, then $U=G_1^\dagger\cdots G_m^\dagger D$. A dimension-d matrix needs at most $d(d-1)/2$ such eliminations. The lab decomposes 4 by 4 matrices into two-level factors, then into single-qubit gates and CNOTs. For two wires, X gates and a CNOT relabel any two basis states so they differ in one bit; the resulting controlled operation uses two CNOTs and local rotations. A Gray-code path connects any two basis strings by single-bit changes. Conjugate the two-level operation by basis-state transpositions along that path until the two strings differ at just one bit, then use a multi-controlled one-qubit gate and undo the transpositions. This proves existence, not optimality: [KAK synthesis](/learn/two-qubit-synthesis/) uses at most three CNOTs for any two-qubit unitary plus local gates. This lab reconstructs the same matrix but can use more. Up to global phase an n-qubit unitary has $4^n-1$ real parameters. Each one- or two-qubit gate supplies at most 15. For fixed n and gate count the possible wire choices are finite, so almost every exact unitary needs at least $(4^n-1)/15$ gates.

</Depth>
