<Depth level="kid">

Imagine a machine that changes two pieces together. Even if the change seems very complicated, you can split it into turns of the separate pieces, a small connecting section, then more separate turns. Surprisingly, the connecting section needs at most three uses of a particular two-piece instruction. Some changes need fewer: turning the two pieces separately needs none, and exchanging them needs three in the usual construction. Change the three adjustable angles in the connecting section and you get different joint transformations. The picture breaks because quantum operations act on complex amplitudes and the separate turns before and after can be arbitrary, not just ordinary mechanical rotations.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Any two-qubit unitary can be written as local one-qubit gates, a central interaction, then more local gates, up to global phase. The central interaction has three angles associated with XX, YY and ZZ and has a circuit using at most three CNOTs. The lab lets you set those angles and compares the whole circuit with the three combined interactions. Some gates need fewer: local H and T need zero, CNOT needs one, a generic nontrivial ZZ rotation can use two, and SWAP needs three. This special two-qubit result is stronger than the general [decomposition](/learn/decomposition/) bound, and it helps compilers translate [two-qubit gates](/learn/two-qubit-gates/).

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Up to global phase, any two-qubit U has the KAK form $(u_1\otimes u_2)A(v_1\otimes v_2)$, where $A=e^{-i(aXX+bYY+cZZ)}$. The three Pauli products commute. The general example implements A using three CNOTs and local rotations, accounting for a global phase of $e^{-i\pi/4}$. Surrounding it by arbitrary local unitaries does not add CNOTs. Some gates need three CNOTs, including SWAP; its canonical exponential at $(\pi/4,\pi/4,\pi/4)$ equals $e^{-i\pi/4}$ times SWAP. The lab accepts independently chosen canonical angles and compares complete matrices; it does not extract the KAK factors from arbitrary input matrices.

</Depth>
