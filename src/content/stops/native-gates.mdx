<Depth level="kid">

Imagine two workshops with different tools. One can connect pieces by pressing them together, while the other uses a different kind of connection. You give both workshops the same finished-shape instructions, but each must translate them into its own available actions. Two short turns may replace one larger turn, and a connection plus a few local turns may replace a different kind of connection. Both workshops can make the same final transformation even though their lists of tasks differ. The picture breaks because quantum gates change amplitudes and their overall phase may differ without changing the observed results. Real workshops also differ in speed and accuracy.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A device has a set of [native gates](/learn/qubit-control/) it can execute directly. In one example basis RZ, SX and CZ are available, while another offers RZ, SX, X and ECR. Two SX gates make X; RZ(90°), SX, RZ(90°) make H up to global phase. H on the target around CZ implements [CNOT](/learn/cnot/). ECR, with different local gates, can implement the same CNOT. Try both bases and compare the complete matrices. These are mathematical illustrations of [two-qubit gate families](/learn/two-qubit-gates/), not calibrated schedules. A [universal](/learn/universal/) native basis can implement other gates exactly or approximately; a compiler uses the particular device's instructions and topology.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Here $SX=HSH$, so $SX^2=X$. $R_Z(\pi/2)SX R_Z(\pi/2)=e^{-i\pi/4}H$. Conjugating CZ by H on the target gives CX. Each native H carries $e^{-i\pi/4}$, so that native CX carries $e^{-i\pi/2}$. In the q0-left ECR convention, CX is X on q0, ECR, then $R_Z(\pi/2)$ on q0 and SX on q1, with no additional global phase. These exact identities illustrate two possible bases; real devices have specific calibrated operations, coupling directions, durations and errors. Consult the target instructions rather than assuming one basis fits every chip.

</Depth>
