<Depth level="kid">

Picture a recipe book. A coin picks a recipe, and each recipe gives qubit A its own state and qubit B its own state. A pair is entangled when no such book can make it.

For two qubits there is a paper trick: write the pair's state as a grid of numbers, then flip B's half. A real state's grid has four special numbers, like odds, never negative. Any book's grid keeps that after the flip, so a negative one rules out every book.

Take a Bell pair (start as 00, a Hadamard gate on A, a CNOT from A to B) with chance x, else random bits. A negative number appears once x is above one third.

Where the picture breaks: the flip is paper work, not something a device does. And the trick is exact only for two qubits, or a qubit and a three-level system.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A mixed state of two qubits is separable if it is a mix of product states, one state for each qubit. Otherwise it is entangled.

A two-qubit [density matrix](/learn/density-matrix/) is a 4 by 4 grid. Flip each of its four 2 by 2 blocks over its diagonal: that flips B's half (the partial transpose). A state's eigenvalues are never negative, and a mix of product states keeps that after the flip, so a negative eigenvalue means entangled. This is the PPT test.

Start as 00, apply a Hadamard gate to A, then a CNOT from A to B: the Bell pair. Make it with chance x, else a random one of 00, 01, 10 and 11: the smallest eigenvalue is (1 − 3x)/4, negative once x is above ⅓. For two qubits, no negative eigenvalue means separable; beyond a qubit and a three-level system, some entangled states pass.

A witness is a quicker check from measuring each qubit alone: below 0 means entangled, 0 or more shows nothing, and each witness catches only some entangled states. A gate on B can blind the Bell pair witness; the PPT eigenvalues do not move.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A two-qubit state $\rho$ is separable if $\rho = \sum_k p_k\,\rho^A_k \otimes \rho^B_k$, a mix of product states; otherwise it is entangled. Deciding separability is NP-hard in general (Gurvits, as in Aaronson's notes).

The partial transpose on B is $\langle ab|\rho^\Gamma|a'b'\rangle = \langle ab'|\rho|a'b\rangle$. For a product mix, $\rho^\Gamma = \sum_k p_k\,\rho^A_k \otimes (\rho^B_k)^T$, and each transpose is a density matrix, so $\rho^\Gamma \ge 0$: a negative eigenvalue proves entanglement (Peres 1996). Gates $U \otimes V$ leave its eigenvalues alone. For $2\times2$ and $2\times3$ systems, $\rho^\Gamma \ge 0$ also shows separability (Horodecki, Horodecki and Horodecki 1996). For larger systems it does not: entangled states that pass exist, and they are bound entangled.

Take the Bell pair $|\Phi^+\rangle$ (from $|00\rangle$: Hadamard on A, then CNOT) with chance $x$, else $I/4$:

$$
\rho_x = x\,|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+| + (1-x)\,I/4.
$$

$\rho_x^\Gamma$ has eigenvalues $\tfrac{1+x}{4}$ three times and $\tfrac{1-3x}{4}$: entangled exactly when $x > \tfrac13$. At $x = \tfrac13$ it is an equal mix of the six pairs $|a\rangle|a^*\rangle$ ($a$ an eigenstate of $X$, $Y$ or $Z$), and smaller $x$ adds $I/4$, so it is separable for $x \le \tfrac13$.

A witness is a Hermitian $W$ with $\operatorname{tr}(W\sigma) \ge 0$ for every separable $\sigma$; $\operatorname{tr}(W\rho) < 0$ shows entanglement. $W = I/2 - |\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+|$ works: a product state has $|\langle\Phi^+|a\,b\rangle|^2 \le \tfrac12$. As $|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+| = \tfrac14(II + XX - YY + ZZ)$, three local settings measure it. Every entangled state has some witness, but none fits all. After $\mathrm{RY}(\beta)$ on B:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(W\rho_x) = \tfrac14\bigl(1 - x(1 + 2\cos\beta)\bigr).
$$

For $\beta \ge 90^\circ$ it is never negative, yet the eigenvalues of $\rho_x^\Gamma$ do not depend on $\beta$.

</Depth>
