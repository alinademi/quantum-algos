<Depth level="kid">

Think of a globe. Put 0 at the North Pole and 1 at the South Pole. Every superposition of 0 and 1 is one spot on the surface.

How far south the spot sits sets the odds. Near the North Pole you almost always read 0. On the equator, 0 and 1 are equally likely. A qubit that starts as 0 sits at the North Pole, and a Hadamard gate moves it to the equator.

How far around the spot sits, east or west, is the phase. It does not change the odds of 0 and 1. It matters later, when amplitudes add up or cancel.

Where the picture breaks: the globe is a map of the qubit's state, not a place in space. And you can't read the spot off one qubit. Measure it and you get just 0 or 1.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A qubit in a pure state is a|0⟩ + b|1⟩, with two complex amplitudes. Two angles place it on a sphere:

- θ (theta) goes from 0° at the top, |0⟩, to 180° at the bottom, |1⟩.
- φ (phi) goes from 0° to 360° around. It is the relative phase: how far b is turned from a in the complex plane.

Why only two? The odds must add up to 1, and turning both amplitudes by the same angle (a global phase) changes nothing you can measure.

The height of the point sets the odds: the chance of 0 is (1 + height)/2. Started as 0, then a Hadamard gate, the qubit is |+⟩, on the equator at height 0: a 50% chance of 0. φ leaves these odds alone, but it changes results in [other bases](/learn/bases/). Opposite points, like |0⟩ and |1⟩, are orthogonal: a measurement along the line through them tells them apart every time. For |0⟩ and |1⟩ that is the usual 0/1 measurement. Points inside the ball are mixed states, like a noisy qubit.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Up to a global phase, every pure state of one qubit can be written as

$$
|\psi\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle
$$

with $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ and $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi)$. Normalization takes away one of the four real parameters in $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$, and the global phase $e^{i\gamma}$, which no measurement sees, takes away another. The two that are left place a point on the unit sphere, the Bloch vector

$$
(x, y, z) = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta).
$$

From the amplitudes, $x = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\bar\alpha\beta$, $y = 2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\bar\alpha\beta$ and $z = |\alpha|^2 - |\beta|^2$. These are the expectation values $\langle X\rangle$, $\langle Y\rangle$, $\langle Z\rangle$, and $|\psi\rangle\langle\psi| = \tfrac12(I + xX + yY + zZ)$. So the odds in the computational basis are $P(0) = \cos^2\tfrac{\theta}{2} = \tfrac12(1 + z)$, and $\varphi$ never changes them.

The half angles make the geometry work. For two states whose points are an angle $\Theta$ apart,

$$
|\langle\psi|\chi\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12(1 + \cos\Theta) = \cos^2\tfrac{\Theta}{2},
$$

so orthogonal states sit at opposite points: $|0\rangle, |1\rangle$ on $\pm z$, $|{\pm}\rangle$ on $\pm x$, $|{\pm i}\rangle$ on $\pm y$. At the poles $\varphi$ has no meaning: at $\theta = 0$ the $|1\rangle$ term is gone, and at $\theta = \pi$, $e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle$ is $|1\rangle$ up to a global phase.

The surface holds pure states only. A mixed state is $\rho = \tfrac12(I + \vec r\cdot\vec\sigma)$ with $|\vec r| < 1$, inside the ball ([density matrices](/learn/density-matrix/)). Every one-qubit unitary turns the sphere as a rotation ([rotation gates](/learn/rotations/)). The sphere is not a store of endless bits: a standard measurement has two outcomes, a [POVM](/learn/povm/) can have more, yet by [Holevo's bound](/learn/holevo-bound/) one qubit, with no entanglement shared beforehand, gives at most one classical bit about its preparation.

</Depth>
