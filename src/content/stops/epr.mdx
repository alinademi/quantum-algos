<Depth level="kid">

Put a pair of gloves in two boxes and send them far apart. Open yours and find a left glove: you know the far one is right, without touching it.

Tiny particles can be paired like that. You can ask each one either of two questions: "up or down?" or "east or west?". Ask both the same question, and the answers always come out opposite. (Their 1935 paper asked where a particle is and how fast it moves. This up-or-down version came later, from David Bohm in 1951.)

In 1935 Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued: if you can know the far answer for sure without touching that particle, it was real all along. Your choice of question can't change a far particle, so both its answers are real at once. Yet quantum theory never gives a particle sure answers to both. So, they said, the theory leaves something out.

The glove picture breaks here: a glove is left or right all along. In quantum theory, these answers are not set before a measurement. Later tests with more questions, the Bell test, showed set answers cannot explain every result.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

EPR used position and momentum. Bohm's 1951 version uses spin, a qubit.

A source sends pairs in the singlet, an [entangled](/learn/entanglement/) state: one particle to Alice, one to Bob, far apart. Each measures spin along Z (upright) or X (on its side). Alone, each result is up or down at random, half the time each. Along the same axis, the two results are always opposite.

So Alice can measure Z and predict Bob's Z result for sure, or measure X and predict his X result. EPR's criterion: a value you can predict for sure, without disturbing the system, is an "element of reality". They also assumed Alice's choice cannot change what is real at Bob. So his Z and X values are both real at once.

No qubit state gives sure results for both. So, EPR concluded, quantum theory is incomplete. Values it leaves out are now called hidden variables.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

EPR's two particles share the idealized wave function

$$
\Psi(x_1, x_2) = h\,\delta(x_1 - x_2 + x_0)
$$

which they wrote as $\int e^{(2\pi i/h)(x_1 - x_2 + x_0)p}\,dp$. It is an eigenstate of $x_1 - x_2$ (value $-x_0$) and of $p_1 + p_2$ (value $0$). These commute, $[x_1 - x_2,\, p_1 + p_2] = 0$, though $[x_2, p_2] = i\hbar$. Measuring $x_1$ or $p_1$ leaves particle 2 in an eigenstate of $x_2$ or $p_2$.

The premises: a value predictable with certainty "without in any way disturbing a system" is an element of reality; in a complete theory "every element of the physical reality must have a counterpart"; and, assumed but not stressed, the far particle has its own real state that no measurement on particle 1 can change. Then $x_2$ and $p_2$ are both real, yet no wave function is an eigenstate of both. So the description is incomplete.

Bohm's spin version uses the singlet, made from $|11\rangle$ by $H$ on qubit 0, then a CNOT from qubit 0 to 1:

$$
|\Psi^-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|01\rangle - |10\rangle\big)
$$

It keeps this form, up to a global phase, with up and down along any axis replacing 0 and 1. For axes $\theta$ apart, both are up, or both down, with probability $\tfrac12\sin^2\tfrac{\theta}{2}$ each, so along one axis results are always opposite. Yet every qubit state has $\langle\sigma_z\rangle^2 + \langle\sigma_x\rangle^2 \le 1$: none is sure of both.

EPR end with "We believe, however, that such a theory is possible", but propose none. Adding missing values is the hidden-variable route. For $Z$ and $X$ alone, four equally likely answer cards reproduce every joint probability. In 1964 Bell showed no local hidden-variable theory matches the singlet at all angles: see the [Bell test](/learn/bell-test/).

</Depth>
