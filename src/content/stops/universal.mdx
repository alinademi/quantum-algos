<Depth level="kid">

Imagine building machines from a box of parts. With parts that can turn by any angle and a part that connects two moving pieces, you can reproduce any allowed transformation of a finite collection of pieces. What if your box contains only a few fixed parts? You cannot reproduce every turn exactly, but a well-chosen box lets you get as close as you need. More parts give you more choices of approximations. This is the difference between exact and approximate universality. The picture breaks because the pieces are rules for changing quantum amplitudes, and some transformations need enormously many rules even when the box is universal.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Arbitrary [one-qubit rotations](/learn/rotations/) plus [CNOT](/learn/cnot/) build any finite-qubit unitary up to global phase. For example H is a Y rotation after a Z half-turn, and CZ is H on the target before and after CNOT. A finite set like [H](/learn/hadamard/), T and CNOT is approximately universal: for any positive error tolerance it can approximate the target. It cannot exactly represent every possible gate because there are only countably many finite words. Try the approximation ladder below and compare the errors at different budgets. [Solovay-Kitaev](/learn/solovay-kitaev/) explains how a suitable alphabet reaches arbitrarily high precision with polylogarithmic length.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Single-qubit gates plus CNOT can synthesize any finite-dimensional qubit unitary up to global phase ([Barenco et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9503016)). H, T and CNOT generate a dense family up to global phase. One example is $R_Y(\pi/2)R_Z(\pi)=-iH$; another is CZ from conjugating CNOT by H on the target. Universality does not promise short circuits for every unitary.

The ideas used here build on [Phase gates](/learn/phase-gates/).

</Depth>
