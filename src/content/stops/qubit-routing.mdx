<Depth level="kid">

Imagine four desks in a row, each holding a special object. A rule permits only objects on neighboring desks to interact. If two far-apart objects need to interact, exchange an object with its neighbor until it sits beside the other one. Perform the desired action, and exchange the objects back if you need their original positions. Better still, place objects that often interact beside each other from the beginning. The extra exchanges cost time, and planning their order matters. The picture breaks because you cannot copy an unknown quantum object to another desk. A swap exchanges its entire state, including any relationship with other objects.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A [device's connectivity](/learn/connectivity/) tells us which physical qubits can directly interact. A good initial placement maps logical qubits that interact often to neighboring sites, reducing SWAPs. On the line 0–1–2–3, a logical CNOT from 0 to 3 can use two SWAPs to move the control's state to site 2, then the neighboring CNOT, then reverse the SWAPs to restore layout. If both CNOT directions are permitted, each [SWAP](/learn/toffoli/) uses three CNOTs. A [transpiler](/learn/transpilation/) can instead keep track of the new permutation. Compare distances and [costs](/learn/circuit-cost/) in the lab; the simpler restore-layout route is exact but not always optimal.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

The coupling graph defines legal multi-qubit interactions; a directed graph may also constrain CNOT direction. A route changes the permutation of logical states across physical sites. For endpoints at distance d on an undirected line, the displayed restore-layout method uses $2(d-1)$ SWAPs plus one neighboring CNOT. Retaining the updated layout or using bridge circuits may reduce cost.

</Depth>
