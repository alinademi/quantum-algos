<Depth level="kid">

Picture a coin spinning on a table. It is not heads or tails yet. When it stops, it lands on one side.

A qubit is a bit like that coin, but you can tilt the odds before it lands. It keeps two numbers called amplitudes, one for 0 and one for 1, and they set the odds. Start a qubit as 0 and send it through a Hadamard gate. Measure many copies, and about half give 0 and half give 1. Each measurement gives just one answer.

Here the coin picture breaks. An amplitude can be negative, so amplitudes can cancel out, and odds never do. Send the qubit through a second Hadamard gate and the two parts that lead to 1 cancel: it gives 0 every time. If it had secretly been 0 or 1 after the first gate, it would still give half and half.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A qubit's state has two amplitudes, one for 0 and one for 1. We write it as $a|0\rangle + b|1\rangle$. Square an amplitude's size to get the chance of that result. The two chances always add up to 100%.

Start as 0 and apply a Hadamard gate. Both amplitudes become $1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.71$, and $0.71^2 \approx 0.5$, so you get 0 half the time and 1 half the time. Each measurement gives just one answer.

Amplitudes can be negative, or even complex, so they can cancel. Apply a second Hadamard gate and you get 0 every time: the two contributions to the amplitude for 1 are $+\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $-\tfrac{1}{2}$, and they cancel. A qubit that was secretly 0 or secretly 1, with 50/50 odds, would still give 50/50. So superposition is not a mix of 0 and 1. You meet this cancelling again at [Interference](/learn/interference/).

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A qubit state is a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle,
$$

with complex amplitudes $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. A measurement in the computational basis gives one outcome: 0 with probability $|\alpha|^2$, 1 with probability $|\beta|^2$ (the Born rule). For example, $\tfrac{1+2i}{3}|0\rangle - \tfrac{2}{3}|1\rangle$ gives 0 with probability $5/9$.

Started as $|0\rangle$, a Hadamard gate gives $|{+}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$; started as $|1\rangle$, it gives $|{-}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)$. Both measure 50/50, yet they are different states. Since $H^2 = I$, a second Hadamard sends $|{+}\rangle$ to $|0\rangle$ and $|{-}\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$ with certainty. In $H|{+}\rangle$ the two contributions to $|1\rangle$, $+\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $-\tfrac{1}{2}$, cancel.

A 50/50 mix of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ is plain odds over two states, and no $\alpha, \beta$ describe it (see [Mixed states](/learn/mixed-states/)). A Hadamard leaves it at 50/50: each branch gives 50/50, and probabilities only add.

Global phase cannot be seen: $e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$ gives the same statistics in every measurement. Relative phase can: $|{+}\rangle$ and $|{-}\rangle$ differ only in the sign of $\beta$, and a Hadamard, then a measurement, tells them apart. Quantum algorithms are built on this [interference](/learn/interference/).

</Depth>
