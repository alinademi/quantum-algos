description: "Trace out one qubit of a pair to get the state of the other. A Bell pair is known exactly, yet each half is fully mixed. Try it live."
answer: "The partial trace turns the density matrix of a pair into one for a single part, ρ_A, which sets the odds of every measurement on that part alone. For half of a Bell pair it gives I/2, the fully mixed state."
myth:
  claim: "If you know a whole system exactly, you know each part exactly."
  truth: "Not always. A Bell pair is one exact state, yet each qubit alone is fully mixed: any test on it gives 50/50. What is known exactly is how the two results relate in every test, not what either part gives."

<Depth level="kid">

Start two qubits as 0. A Hadamard gate on the first, then a CNOT, makes a Bell pair: an entangled pair whose results always match, 00 or 11.

Now ignore the second qubit and test only the first. Whatever test you pick, its two results come up with equal chance. A qubit with a state of its own always has some test that gives the same result every time. This one has none. We call it fully mixed: plain odds, and nothing is more unknown for a qubit.

The rule for ignoring a qubit is the partial trace. It turns the description of the pair into a description of one part.

Where the picture breaks: cut a photo in half and each half still shows something exact. Here the pair is known exactly, yet each half is as unknown as a qubit can be.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

Start two qubits as 0. A Hadamard gate on the first, then a CNOT, makes the Bell pair (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2.

Its density matrix is a 4 by 4 grid: each entry is the amplitude of a row's ket times that of a column's ket (in general the column's amplitude is complex-conjugated). Four entries are ½, for rows and columns 00 and 11. The rest are 0.

To get the first qubit A alone, trace out B. Cut the grid into four 2 by 2 blocks and add the two diagonal entries of each block. Top left: ½ + 0 = ½. Bottom right: 0 + ½ = ½. The other two give 0. So A's grid is I/2, the fully mixed state: any test on A alone gives 50/50. The two ½ entries that link 00 and 11 sit off the diagonals of their blocks, so the trace drops them.

Without the CNOT, A is |+⟩ and B is 0. A's grid is ½ in all four entries, and the +/− test gives + every time.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

For a pair $AB$ with density matrix $\rho_{AB}$, the partial trace over $B$ is the linear map fixed by

$$
\operatorname{tr}_B(M \otimes N) = \operatorname{tr}(N)\, M.
$$

In the basis $|ab\rangle$, $(\rho_A)_{aa'} = \sum_b \langle ab|\rho_{AB}|a'b\rangle$: cut $\rho_{AB}$ into 2 by 2 blocks, one per $(a, a')$, and take each block's trace. It is the one operator with $\operatorname{tr}[(M \otimes I)\rho_{AB}] = \operatorname{tr}(M\rho_A)$ for every $M$, so $\rho_A$ holds the odds of every measurement on $A$ alone. It is also what $A$ is left with if $B$ is measured in any orthonormal basis and the result is ignored: $\rho_A = \sum_b (I \otimes \langle b|)\,\rho_{AB}\,(I \otimes |b\rangle)$. Nothing done to $B$ alone changes it, as long as $A$'s runs are not sorted by $B$'s results ([no-signaling](/learn/no-signaling/)). Sorted by $B$'s 0/1 result, $A$ of $|\Phi^+\rangle$ is $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$; averaged over $B$'s results it is still $\rho_A$.

Take $|\Phi^+\rangle = (|00\rangle + |11\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Since $\operatorname{tr}_B(|ab\rangle\langle a'b'|) = \delta_{bb'}\,|a\rangle\langle a'|$, the cross terms $|00\rangle\langle 11|$ and $|11\rangle\langle 00|$ vanish and

$$
\rho_A = \tfrac12\big(|0\rangle\langle 0| + |1\rangle\langle 1|\big) = I/2,
$$

with purity $\operatorname{tr}\rho_A^2 = \tfrac12$ while the pair has purity 1.

The tool's pair, started as $|00\rangle$, then $R_Y$ on $A$ with $\sin^2(\theta/2) = p$, then a CNOT, is $\sqrt{1-p}\,|00\rangle + \sqrt p\,|11\rangle$. It gives $\rho_A = \mathrm{diag}(1-p,\, p)$, pure only at $p = 0$ or $1$. Without the CNOT, $\rho_A$ is pure.

For a pure pair, $\rho_A$ is pure exactly when the pair is a product, and $\rho_A$ and $\rho_B$ share their nonzero eigenvalues, the squared coefficients of the [Schmidt decomposition](/learn/schmidt/).

</Depth>
