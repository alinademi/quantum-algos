<Depth level="kid">

Flip a coin and cover it with your hand. It has already landed, heads or tails, but you do not know which. You only know the chances: half heads, half tails.

A computer bit is 0 or 1. Call heads 0 and tails 1, and the covered coin is a random bit: a bit you know only by its chances. We write the two chances as a pair, the chance of 0 first. A fair coin is ½ and ½. A trick coin that lands heads three times out of four is ¾ and ¼. The chances are never below zero, and they add up to 1.

Lift your hand and you see one side, never the chances. To see them, flip many times and count.

A qubit grows out of this picture, but here the coin picture breaks. A qubit has amplitudes, not chances, and amplitudes can cancel out. Chances never do.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A bit is 0 or 1. A random bit is one you know only by its chances. Its probability vector lists them, the chance of 0 first: a fair coin is $(0.5, 0.5)$, and a bit that gives 0 three times in four is $(0.75, 0.25)$. No entry is negative, and the entries add up to 1.

The sure bits are $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, written $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. Every random bit is a weighted sum of them: $(0.75, 0.25) = 0.75|0\rangle + 0.25|1\rangle$.

Look and you see 0 or 1, never the vector. Your vector then becomes $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$: the bit did not change, but what you know did. The vector shows only in the counts over many flips.

A [qubit](/learn/qubit/) keeps the vector, but each entry is an amplitude. The chance of a result is $|\text{amplitude}|^2$. Amplitudes can be negative or complex, so they can cancel; chances cannot.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

Take a bit with classical states $\Sigma = \{0, 1\}$. A probabilistic state is a column vector

$$
p = (p_0, p_1)^{\mathsf T} = p_0|0\rangle + p_1|1\rangle,
$$

with $p_0, p_1 \ge 0$ and $p_0 + p_1 = 1$. Here $|0\rangle = (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ and $|1\rangle = (0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ are the standard basis vectors. All probability vectors of a bit fill the line segment from $|0\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$, whose two ends are the only deterministic states.

Measuring gives $a$ with probability $p_a$, and the description becomes $|a\rangle$: what you know changes, not the bit. One shot never reveals $p$. Over $N$ independent flips the count of 1s is binomial, with mean $Np_1$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{Np_0p_1}$, so the observed share of 1s closes in on $p_1$ only like $1/\sqrt{N}$.

A [qubit](/learn/qubit/) keeps the vector and the basis but changes the entries and the norm:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle,
$$

with complex $\alpha, \beta$ and $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1.$ A measurement gives 0 with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and 1 with probability $|\beta|^2$ (the Born rule). "Entries add up to 1" (1-norm equal to 1) becomes "squared magnitudes add up to 1" (2-norm equal to 1). Probability vectors change by [stochastic matrices](/learn/stochastic-matrices/), qubit states by unitary matrices.

Same odds do not mean same state. Started as $|0\rangle$, a Hadamard gate gives $|+\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$, which reads 0 or 1 at 50/50, like a fair coin. A second Hadamard gives $|0\rangle$ every time, because the two contributions to the amplitude of 1 cancel. A qubit set to $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ by a fair coin flip gives 50/50 after one Hadamard or two: each case alone gives 50/50, so nothing cancels. More at [Superposition](/learn/superposition/).

</Depth>
