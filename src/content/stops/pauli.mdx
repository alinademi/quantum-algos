<Depth level="kid">

Picture an arrow fixed at the center of a ball. Spin the ball halfway around a line through its center and the arrow changes direction. There are three special lines: one left to right, one front to back, and one top to bottom. A half turn around each line is a different operation. The turn around the left-to-right line flips an arrow pointing up to one pointing down, and vice versa.

Repeat any one of the turns and the arrow returns. The picture breaks because the arrow is a way of drawing an ideal qubit's amplitudes; there is no little spinning object inside the qubit.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

The Pauli gates X, Y and Z are half turns of the [Bloch sphere](/learn/bloch/) around its x, y and z axes. X is the quantum NOT: it exchanges the 0 and 1 amplitudes. Z leaves the 0 amplitude unchanged and changes the sign of the 1 amplitude. Y exchanges them, multiplying the new 0 amplitude by −i and the new 1 amplitude by i.

For an ideal qubit, all three gates undo themselves: applying X, Y or Z twice gives exactly the original amplitudes. Start in |0⟩ and X gives |1⟩. Start in |+⟩ and Z gives |−⟩. The 0/1 odds need not change.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

X has rows $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$; Y has rows $(0,-i)$ and $(i,0)$; Z is diagonal with entries $(1,-1)$. Each is Hermitian and unitary, and $X^2=Y^2=Z^2=I$. For distinct Paulis, $XY=iZ$ and $YX=-iZ$ (cyclic permutations give the other products).

With $R_P(\theta)=e^{-i\theta P/2}$, we have $R_P(\pi)=-iP$. The factor is global phase for this unconditional gate. Each Pauli conjugates the Bloch vector by a half turn about its axis: X sends $(x,y,z)$ to $(x,-y,-z)$, Y to $(-x,y,-z)$, and Z to $(-x,-y,z)$. This connects the [gate](/learn/gates/) matrix with the Bloch picture.

</Depth>
