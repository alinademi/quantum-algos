<Depth level="kid">

Picture a machine that flips a hidden coin before it makes each qubit. Heads, it makes 0. Tails, it makes +, with equal amplitudes for 0 and 1. You get the qubits, never the coin.

A density matrix is a grid of four numbers that holds all you can learn by measuring these qubits. Its diagonal gives the odds of reading 0 and 1: here ¾ and ¼.

For one qubit, the grid is also a point in a ball. The same state every time puts the point on the skin. Mixing different states pulls it inside. At the center, every basis gives its two results with equal chance.

Where the picture breaks: with everyday things, you could check each one to learn the recipe. Here, a machine that makes 0 or 1 by a fair coin and one that makes + or − by a fair coin give the same grid. In quantum theory, no measurement can tell them apart, not even on many qubits, if each is made with its own coin flip.

</Depth>

<Depth level="student">

A density matrix ρ ("rho") describes a qubit as a 2 by 2 grid. For a pure state |ψ⟩ = a|0⟩ + b|1⟩, ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|. Its diagonal holds |a|² and |b|², the odds of 0 and 1, which add to 1. The other two entries show the superposition of 0 and 1 and its relative phase; a plain mix of 0 and 1 has 0 there, but 0 there does not mean no superposition went in: half |+⟩, half |−⟩ also has 0 there.

Making |0⟩ with chance ½, else |+⟩, mixes the grids the same way: ρ = ½|0⟩⟨0| + ½|+⟩⟨+|. Its diagonal is ¾ and ¼, and both other entries are ¼.

No ρ gives a negative chance in any measurement. For one qubit, each ρ is a point in a ball of radius 1. Pure states sit on the surface, the [Bloch sphere](/learn/bloch/); a mix of different states sits inside, at the weighted average of its points. Half |0⟩, half |1⟩ and half |+⟩, half |−⟩ both give the center, I/2: same ρ, same odds in every measurement on the qubit. Half of an entangled pair also has a ρ, with no coin involved.

</Depth>

<Depth level="engineer">

A density matrix is a matrix $\rho$ with $\operatorname{tr}\,\rho = 1$ and $\rho \ge 0$: Hermitian with no negative eigenvalues. A pure state gives the rank-one projector $\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, and a global phase drops out. Preparing $|\psi_k\rangle$ with probability $p_k$ gives

$$
\rho = \textstyle\sum_k p_k\,|\psi_k\rangle\langle\psi_k|.
$$

A measurement with projectors $\Pi_m$ gives $p(m) = \operatorname{tr}(\Pi_m\rho)$, so the diagonal holds the odds in the computational basis, and a unitary acts as $\rho \mapsto U\rho U^\dagger$. Ensembles with the same $\rho$ give the same odds in every measurement on that system, so $\rho$ holds all that measurement of it can reveal. For $N$ independently prepared copies the joint state is $\rho^{\otimes N}$; if the preparations are correlated, as with one coin toss for all copies, the same $\rho$ does not fix the joint state. By the spectral theorem every $\rho$ can be written as a mix of orthogonal pure states, its eigenvectors, weighted by its eigenvalues. $\rho$ describes a state whatever its origin: a preparation list or part of a larger pure state.

For a qubit,

$$
\rho = \tfrac12\,(I + xX + yY + zZ),
$$

with $x = \operatorname{tr}(X\rho)$ and so on, so $\rho_{00} = \tfrac12(1 + z)$ and $\rho_{10} = \tfrac12(x + iy)$. The eigenvalues are $\tfrac12(1 \pm r)$, where $r$ is the length of the Bloch vector $\mathbf r = (x, y, z)$, so $\rho \ge 0$ exactly when $r \le 1$: the states fill the Bloch ball. Pure states form the surface. Mixing averages Bloch vectors, $\mathbf r = \sum_k p_k \mathbf r_k$, so mixed states lie inside, and the purity $\operatorname{tr}\,\rho^2 = \tfrac12(1 + r^2)$ falls from 1 to ½ at the center, $I/2$, the completely mixed state. Measuring along a unit vector $\mathbf n$ gives the result at $+\mathbf n$ with probability $\tfrac12(1 + \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n)$, and a unitary rotates the ball.

Half of a Bell pair sits at the center ([partial trace](/learn/partial-trace/)), and noise shrinks, squashes or shifts the ball ([noise channels](/learn/noise-channels/)). The ball is special to qubits: with three or more levels, the same picture (8 numbers for three levels) gives a shape that is not a ball: pure states cover only part of its surface.

</Depth>
