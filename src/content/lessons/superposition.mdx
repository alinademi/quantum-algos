## What it is

<div data-depth="kid">

Picture a coin spinning on a table. It is not heads or tails yet. When it stops, it lands on one side.

A qubit is a bit like that coin, but you can tilt the odds before it lands. The coin is only a picture: real qubits can also cancel each other out, and coins never do that.

</div>

<div data-depth="student">

A qubit's state has two amplitudes: one for 0 and one for 1. The chance of a result is the amplitude's size, squared: |amplitude|². A minus sign or phase does not change that chance. The two chances always add up to 100%.

A Hadamard gate turns a plain 0 into an even split: a 50% chance of 0 and a 50% chance of 1.

</div>

<div data-depth="engineer">

A qubit state is a unit vector in a two-dimensional complex space:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
$$

The Hadamard gate maps $|0\rangle$ to $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$. Global phase can't be observed, but the relative phase between $\alpha$ and $\beta$ drives interference, which is what quantum algorithms use.

</div>
